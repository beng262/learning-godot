Little Hours from A to Z

Twenty-six things the game does, from Animals to Zzz. For each: what the player does, the rule underneath, the code that ships, and a live tutorial in which you rebuild the rule yourself, small enough to finish in ten minutes.

What this page is for

  • seeing how a finished game is made of small rules, each of which you could write
  • reading real Godot code with its reason beside it, not a toy written to make a point
  • rebuilding each rule in the page, a step at a time, with your code running as you type
  • finding which chapter of the book teaches the Godot idea a rule leans on

How to read it

Little Hours is a cosy life game: a young woman moves into a small seaside town, keeps a tiny house, and gets to know 15 townspeople over about two hundred days. Nothing in it is hard to program. What makes it a game is how many small rules there are and how carefully each was tuned.

Every entry has the same four parts. What the player sees is the rule from outside, as you would describe it to a friend. The rule is the same thing with its numbers. The code is the function that does it, quoted from the game as it is today – the build pulls it from the project, so it cannot fall behind. And Build it live is a tutorial: the same rule at a tenth of the size, written by you.

The tutorials run on the book's interpreter, so they have no scene tree, no files and no sprites. That is less of a loss than it sounds. Almost every rule in a game is arithmetic on a few numbers and a dictionary, and that part runs here exactly as it does in Godot. Where a rule needs the engine, the entry says which part, and which chapter explains it.

AAnimals: a dog at your heel, a cat on her own terms

Each animal has one word, its mode, that says what it wants right now, and a target it walks towards. Everything that makes the dog feel like a dog is in how the target is chosen and how fast it is allowed to go – above all, that a follower has to be quicker than the one it follows.

What the player sees

The dog comes with you through every door. It walks a little behind you and to one side, breaks into a run when it has been left behind, and settles when you stand still. The cat keeps to one room of the house – a different one on different days – and wanders it by herself. Tap either of them for a card of things to do: a fuss, a treat, a brush, a thrown ball, a trick to teach (one try a day), a nap. You can name them both and pick the dog's breed, and the coat of the dog that was drawn for this game. Now and then, unasked, the dog tears across the room for no reason at all, and late at night it may fall asleep where it stands.

The rule

The idea to take away is a state machine with one target. The animal does not have a separate piece of code for fetching, for the zoomies, for coming over to be brushed: each of those sets a mode, a target and whether to run, and the same few lines of walking carry it out.

The code

Both animals are one script, Pet.gd, a Node2D with a sprite under it (Chapter L). The numbers that make the dog behave sit at the top as constants, and each carries the story of how it got its value. This is the most important one:

Pet.gd · lines 173–180

## How fast a dog runs. Faster than you, which it was not.
##
## "I want the dog to run when he is away from me" — he was trying. Both dog
## specs ran at 260 and Player.SPEED is 280, so a dog that fell behind could
## never close the gap however hard it ran: the distance only ever grew while
## you were moving, and shrank when you stopped. A follower has to be quicker
## than the thing it follows or it is not following, it is being left.
const CATCH_UP := 330.0

Read the comment before the number. The dog was running, as asked, at 260 – and you walk at 280. A gap that only shrinks when you stop is not following. Nothing about the code was wrong; one number was smaller than another. When something that chases feels broken, compare the two speeds first.

Where it runs to had a bug of the same kind:

Pet.gd · lines 206–214

## Where a following animal walks: this far BEHIND you, and a little to one side.
##
## The heel spot used to be a fixed 70px to your left, whichever way you were
## facing – so on every walk west the dog was 70px ahead of you, standing in the
## next step, and at rest it sat close enough to touch. "My lovely dog always
## gets in the way, I want him to have a bit more space" (3 Nov). Behind is
## measured from the way you last walked, so it stays behind when you turn.
const HEEL_BACK := 120.0
const HEEL_SIDE := 36.0

A fixed offset – always 70 pixels to your left – is in front of you whenever you walk that way. So the game remembers the direction you last walked, as a vector of length one, and takes the spot behind that. A vector pointing the way you are going, multiplied by a distance and subtracted from where you are: that is all "behind you" means (Chapter L).

Then how fast. A walk and a run were not enough:

Pet.gd · lines 891–897

## How fast to go this frame, at a walk or a run.
func _speed_now(key: String) -> float:
	var speeds: Dictionary = _spec["speed"]
	var speed: float = speeds[key]
	if key == "walk" and mode == "follow" and _lead_speed > speed / KEEP_UP:
		speed = minf(_lead_speed * KEEP_UP, float(speeds.get("run", speed)))
	return speed

The speeds come out of the animal's own table (_spec, one dictionary for the dog and another for the cat – Chapter V). The if is the fix for a dog that walked at 200 beside somebody walking at 280: it lost ground, ran to win it back, walked, lost ground again. So while it is following and you are moving, its walk becomes your speed times KEEP_UP – a little faster than you, so it closes up – and minf stops that ever passing its run.

Last, the thrown ball, which shows why one target is enough:

Pet.gd · lines 463–470

## Sprint to a thrown toy. Deliberately not a mode of its own: it sets a target
## and lets the normal wander logic take over once the dog gets there, so the
## fetch ends by itself instead of needing a state to be cleared.
func fetch_to(spot: Vector2) -> void:

	_target = _free_spot(_clamp(spot))

There is no "fetch" mode. Fetching is wandering with the first destination chosen for it and _running switched on, so when the dog arrives the ordinary wander code is already in charge and nothing has to be cleared. Main.gd throws the ball by picking the spot, calling this, and starting a one-shot timer – get_tree().create_timer(3.4) – whose timeout sets the mode back to "follow" (Chapter T). The zoomies call the very same function with a silly destination.

BBeginning: a few questions, then the keys

A new life starts with a short run of questions, and one function writes the answers into the save. The idea worth stealing is what happens next: every answer moves a real number somewhere in the game. A choice that only changes a label is a promise the game does not keep.

What the player sees

The house appears for a moment, and then a card asks who you are: your name, how you look, whether you are hoping to meet anybody, a few small "what would you do" questions, and last of all how hard you want it – Cozy, Balanced or Hustle. A summary reads your answers back, you press Start living, and the whole town is shown from above while the camera sinks towards one house. Then you are standing in it, on the evening of the day you moved in. For the first days your character thinks aloud, one thought at a time, and each thought waits until you have done the thing it is about.

modecosy, story or hustle – three numbers each, below.

Nothing above is stored as a setting screen's worth of switches. It is a handful of strings in the save – "mode": "cosy" – and small functions that turn a string into a number at the moment the number is needed.

The code

The questions are cards in Overlays.gd, and they keep what you chose in a dictionary called _create until the life starts. How an answer is recorded was once a bug:

Overlays.gd · lines 2901–2913

## recompute the totals from the record.

Adding one to a total as you tap is fine while the player can only go forwards. Give them a Back button and the answer they changed is still in the total. So the card stores which answer each question got, keyed by the question, and _rebuild_tallies() counts from scratch every time. Storing a fact and working out the total from it is the same habit as the clock's (C): the total cannot be wrong, because nobody keeps it.

The clock multiplies delta by the day scale every frame, which is all it takes to make a cosy day last ten minutes and not eight.

The opening itself is a different kind of code: a function that takes several seconds to run.

Opening.gd · lines 118–128

And not _cancel in the loop's condition is the whole of the Skip button: pressing it sets one bool, and every wait in the function gives up at once.

CClock: the day, its phases and night

One number, counted up every frame, is the whole of time in Little Hours. The phase of the day, the clock in the corner, the colour of the light and whether the lamps are on are all worked out from it when they are needed – none of them is stored.

What the player sees

A day runs from 06:00 to 22:00 and takes eight minutes of play. The light warms through the morning, goes gold in the evening, rose at dusk and indigo at night, and the lamps come up as it darkens. At 22:00 the clock stops: night lasts until you go to bed, so nothing is ever missed by being slow.

The rule

ThingHow it is worked out
clock_tSeconds into today, from 0 to DAY_LENGTH (480). The only thing that is counted.
day_fraction()clock_t / DAY_LENGTH, kept between 0 and 1. Everything else reads this.
phase()Morning below 0.2, Daytime below 0.55, Evening below 0.75, Night after.
clock_text()The fraction stretched over 06:00 to 22:00 – 360 to 1320 minutes – and written as HH:MM.
The lightA Gradient of seven colours sampled at the fraction, given to a CanvasModulate that tints the whole world.

The idea to take away is derived state. If the phase were a variable of its own, something would have to remember to change it at the right moment, and one day it would disagree with the clock. Because it is a function of clock_t, it cannot.

The code

Time passes in the _process() of Game, the autoload that holds everything that changes while you play (Chapter U). delta is the time since the last frame, so the clock runs at the same speed on a fast phone and a slow one (Chapter I):

Game.gd · lines 440–448

func _process(delta: float) -> void:
	if night_held:
		return
	clock_t += delta * mode_day_scale()
	if clock_t >= DAY_LENGTH:
		clock_t = DAY_LENGTH
		night_held = true
	_tick_farm()
	_tick_texts(delta)

When the day runs out the clock is pinned to its last second and night_held turns everything off, including the farm and the phone, until the player sleeps. Then the readings, each a few lines:

Game.gd · lines 1258–1263

func phase() -> String:
	var f := day_fraction()
	if f < 0.2: return "Morning"
	if f < 0.55: return "Daytime"
	if f < 0.75: return "Evening"
	return "Night"

Game.gd · lines 1268–1271

func clock_text() -> String:
	# 06:00 at sunrise through 22:00 at nightfall.
	var mins := int(lerpf(360.0, 1320.0, day_fraction()))
	return "%02d:%02d" % [(mins / 60) % 24, mins % 60]

lerpf(a, b, t) is the workhorse: the value a share t of the way from a to b. Minutes are kept as one whole number and split with / and % only at the end – integer division throws away the remainder, which is exactly what an hour is. "%02d" pads to two digits, so nine o'clock reads 09:00.

The light is the same trick with colours instead of minutes. A Gradient holds colours at positions between 0 and 1; asking it for the colour at day_fraction() gives the tint for this moment:

DDecorating: hold, drag, let go

Everything you rearrange is kept as a short list in the save – which piece, and where its middle is – and the room on screen is built from that list every time you walk in. A drag moves only the picture; the drop is the moment the save is told. Keeping those two apart, and knowing exactly when they meet, is the whole mechanic.

What the player sees

Press on a piece of furniture and drag, and it comes with your finger; or press and keep still for a third of a second and it is picked up where it stands. Let go and it settles into place. Hold one of the room's own pieces and let go without moving it, and a picker opens to change what it looks like. From your own card you can add new pieces from a catalogue, sorted under tabs, and change the walls and the floor – each room keeps its own look. Whatever you put lower down the room is drawn in front, so a lamp set down before the sofa really is in front of it.

The rule

ThingWhat it is
decorFor each room, a list of the pieces you added: {uid, k, x, y}. uid is a name made up for that one piece, k is its key in the catalogue (the art's file name), x and y are its middle.
obj_pos, obj_artFor the furniture that came with the room, only what you changed: where you moved a piece to, and which catalogue piece it now looks like. A piece with no entry is as it was built.
surfacesFor each room, a wall key and a floor key. No entry means the room's own.
HOLD_TIME0.32 seconds. A press on a piece, held this long, picks it up.
DRAG_START6 pixels. A press that began on a piece and has moved this far picks it up at once, without waiting.
HOLD_SLOP14 pixels. A press on bare floor that has slid this far is no longer a hold.
SNAP6 pixels. A dropped piece lands on the nearest point of a 6-pixel grid (DRAG_SNAP is the same number for the room's own furniture).
A tap, not a moveA built-in piece let go less than 4 pixels from where it was picked up has not been moved: the picker opens.
A new pieceAdded to the save at (384, 900), and the room's pieces are built again.
DepthA piece's z_index is the y of the bottom of its art. Larger is nearer the front.

Notice what is not saved for the room's own furniture: the furniture. The room is described once, as data, for every player (Chapter V); a save holds only the differences. An untouched house costs a save nothing, and a room that is redesigned in a later version still works with an old save.

The code

The pieces you add live on their own layer, DecorLayer.gd, as plain Sprite2D children. The layer keeps a small entry for each – its uid, its sprite and the rectangle it covers – and moving one is three calls: begin_drag, drag_to over and over, and this:

DecorLayer.gd · lines 131–150

func end_drag() -> void:
	if _dragging_uid == "":
		return
	var e := _entry(_dragging_uid)
	if not e.is_empty():
		var s: Sprite2D = e["sprite"]
		var snapped := Vector2(
			roundf(s.position.x / float(SNAP)) * float(SNAP),
			roundf(s.position.y / float(SNAP)) * float(SNAP),
		)
		s.position = snapped
		# The snap can move it a few pixels down or up, past somebody's feet.
		s.z_index = int(s.position.y + Assets.base_offset_tex(s.texture, int(s.scale.x)))
		var size: Vector2 = (e["rect"] as Rect2).size
		e["rect"] = Rect2(snapped - size * 0.5, size)
		Game.move_decor(room, _dragging_uid, snapped)
		Sfx.play("pop", -14.0)
	_dragging_uid = ""
	changed.emit()
	queue_redraw()

Dividing by the grid size, rounding, and multiplying back is how you snap any number to a grid. The depth is worked out again after the snap, and the comment says why: a few pixels up or down can be the difference between in front of somebody's feet and behind them. Only then does Game.move_decor() write the new place into the save. Until this function runs, the save does not know the piece has moved – which was once a bug:

DecorLayer.gd · lines 65–73

The fix is one rule: a drag always ends through end_drag(), however it ends. (set_editing is from when decorating was a mode you switched on. Main.gd still has that switch in _set_editing(), but the button for it has gone, and holding a piece is the gesture now – which is why the drag functions take a force argument.)

What turns a press into a pick-up is a timer counted by hand in Main.gd:

The same finger does three jobs in this game – tap to walk, tap to use, hold to move – so a press has to wait before it knows which it is. _press_t adds up delta while the finger is down (Chapter I); at HOLD_TIME it asks your own decor first, then the room's furniture, whether there is a piece under the press. or stops at the first one that says yes, which is how "your pieces get first refusal" is written. The checks at the top are there because a room can be rebuilt while a finger is still down.

For the room's own furniture the save is read like this:

	var entry: Dictionary = per.get(room, {})

A function that returns a position needs a way to say "there isn't one". EEvenings and events: who comes to the door

Every so often Little Hours does something small on its own: a doorbell, a cat with a gift, the dog losing its mind. Once in a while it does something bigger – somebody you know turns up in the evening and asks what you are doing tonight. Both come from one timer, and the bigger one is guarded by a short list of conditions that all have to be true at once.

What the player sees

Every half minute or so, a line of text appears and a little sound plays: friends have arrived with snacks, the cat has brought a leaf, your partner has wandered over, the dog is running laps for no reason. None of it asks for anything. Late in the day, though, if you are indoors and you have got to know somebody properly, there is sometimes a knock and a card with their name on it and four ways to spend the evening. You can have only one of these a day, and never with somebody whose story you have barely begun.

The farm is a grid of twelve patches, and each one is a small dictionary that is dug, planted, watered and picked in that order. Nothing counts down: a patch only remembers when it was planted, and a rule asks whether enough time has passed. That one idea is worth stealing for anything that takes a while.

GGhost in the basement: an evening visitor

Somebody else lives in the house, and she is not on a timetable. Whether she is there is the answer to three small questions – the right room, late enough, anything left to say – that the game asks every twelve seconds. The idea worth stealing is the shape: a gate built from little yes-or-no functions, each of which you can read on its own.

What the player sees

Go down to the basement late in the day and, after a moment, a pale figure fades in somewhere across the floor and hangs there, rising and falling a little. Tap her and a card opens with everything she has told you so far and a button to say hello. One hello an evening: she tells you one more thing, or on some evenings nothing much at all, and soon afterwards she fades. Come back tomorrow. A few of the things she has to say will not come until something has happened elsewhere in town first.

The rule

All she remembers about you is one small dictionary in the save, {"met": false, "heard": 0, "day": 0}: whether you have met, how many evenings you have spent with her, and the day of the last one. Three more flags are added to it late in her story. Everything else is worked out from that and the clock.

Is this one of her rooms?GhostData.haunts(): every room of the house that the map puts below the ground floor. Today that is the basement. If a map had no such room, OLD_HAUNTS – four rooms above ground – stands in.

Has she anything for you?On an ordinary evening, ghost_can_hear(): heard is below the length of her STORY list, and day – the day you last listened – is before today. So one piece a day, however long you stand there.

Each has a function of its own that answers "is it due?", and each sets a flag when it has happened, so it happens once. The first needs every ordinary evening heard, and a certain keepsake shown to the person who can read it (see K). The second needs the first, and any one townsperson's story heard as far as visit 23 (FILM_VISIT). The third needs the first, must not jump ahead of the second while that is waiting, and needs one particular neighbour's story heard to its end. None of the three has the once-a-day limit. After the third she does not appear again. What is said in them is for you to find out.

Each of the townspeople has a story, told a chapter at a time, and a chapter opens only when two different things are both true: enough days have gone by, and you have spoken to that person on enough of them. Time alone does not do it and visiting alone does not do it – which is what turns a list of text into somebody you get to know.

What the player sees

There are 15 people in town, each standing in the room they work or live in. Walk up to one and tap them. If they have something new for you, you hear it, and you can answer in one of four ways. Come back the same afternoon and it is small talk; come back the next morning and it is still small talk, because the next chapter is days off. Somebody you see often tells you more, sooner. Somebody you never visit stays a stranger however long you live there. When a person has more to say but is not ready, you are told which it is: that you do not know them well enough yet, or that whatever they are working up to has not happened yet.

The room is bigger than the screen, the screen could be any shape, and every pixel of the art has to land on a whole number of screen pixels or it goes soft. Little Hours solves all three the same way: pick a whole-number scale first, and work out everything else from it.

What the player sees

The game is played sideways. The picture fills the whole screen on a phone or a laptop with no bars at the sides, and the art is crisp rather than blurred. You see part of the room, and the view slides along as you walk and stops at the walls: you never see past the edge of the world. A small room is never made smaller than what the camera shows. ## A decent integer hash, for the two places the phone needs to decide
## something once per day and stick to it.
##
## This was String.hash() on "ring:%d" and "who:%d", which is fine for a
## dictionary and wrong for this: those strings differ in one character, so
## their low bits move together. With fifteen people to choose between it
## looked random; with two, every single day that rang picked the same one of
## them. The mixer below is the usual splitmix-style avalanche — each output
## bit depends on all the input bits, which is the property actually wanted.
static func _mix(n: int) -> int:
	var x: int = n & 0xFFFFFFFF
	x = ((x ^ (x >> 16)) * 0x7feb352d) & 0xFFFFFFFF
	x = ((x ^ (x >> 15)) * 0x846ca68b) & 0xFFFFFFFF
	return (x ^ (x >> 16)) & 0xFFFFFFFF

The first version hashed strings such as "ring:12". Those differ in one character, so their low bits move together, and with only two people to choose between every ringing day picked the same one. A good hash is an avalanche: change one input bit and about half the output bits change. The two multiply-and-shift lines do that. The & 0xFFFFFFFF keeps the number to 32 bits so it behaves the same everywhere.

A whole life in Little Hours is one dictionary, written out as text whenever something in it changes. Reading it back is the careful half: a save file is treated as a claim, checked field by field against a brand new life, so a damaged file gives you a slightly emptier game instead of no game at all.

What the player sees

There is no save button. Whatever you do is kept, and it is still there if the phone closes the game while you answer a message. Settings holds three separate lives to move between. VVoices and sound: every noise made from numbers

There is not one audio file in Little Hours. A sound is a short recipe – a pitch, a length, a shape – and a function that turns the recipe into a list of numbers for the speaker. A person's voice is the same thing one size smaller: a dictionary of seven values.

What the player sees

Or rather hears. Doors thud, coins ring, the dog barks, and a slow, quiet chord sits under everything. When somebody talks, their line types itself out with a little blip every letter or two, and each person's blip is their own: one is low and unhurried, another is bright and never stops. The narrator makes no sound at all. In Settings there is a switch and a volume slider for effects, the same for music, and a separate switch for the voices.

The rule

Sound, to a computer, is a long list of numbers between −1 and 1: where the speaker cone should be, many thousands of times a second. Each number is a sample. Play the list at the right speed and you hear it. So anything that can fill a list with numbers can make a sound.

A voice profile reads like a description of a person. f is the pitch in hertz. wave is the shape of one cycle, which is what makes a flute different from a buzzer. dur is how long one blip lasts, in seconds. step is how many letters go by between blips: 1 is chatter, 3 is somebody who says little. wob is how far the pitch wanders, soft rounds off the harsher shapes, and db is the level.

The idea to take away is describe it, do not store it. Twenty effects, a piece of music and a voice for everybody in town come to a few hundred lines of code and no files at all – and changing one is editing a number.

## A sine that glides from f0 to f1 with an exponential fade.
func _sweep(f0: float, f1: float, dur: float, curve: float = 4.0) -> PackedFloat32Array:
	var n := _len(dur)
	var out := PackedFloat32Array()
	out.resize(n)
	var phase := 0.0
	for i in range(n):
		var t := float(i) / float(n)
		var f: float = lerpf(f0, f1, t)
		phase += TAU * f / float(RATE)
		out[i] = sin(phase) * pow(1.0 - t, curve)
	return out

Read it as a loop that writes one sample at a time. t runs from 0 to 1 across the sound. phase is how far round its cycle the wave has got: each sample moves it on by TAU * f / RATE, a full turn being TAU, so a higher f turns faster and sounds higher. Because f is itself blended from f0 to f1 as t grows, the pitch glides. Then pow(1.0 - t, curve) fades it: 1 at the start, 0 at the end, falling away faster the bigger curve is. Keeping a running phase, not working out sin(TAU * f * time) afresh, is what lets the pitch change without a jump in the wave.

XeXtra small talk: the town's mind, held in a filter

Every chapter in Little Hours is written by a person and stays written. What a small language model adds, on the phone itself, is the rest of a conversation: the moments when somebody has nothing new to tell you. The interesting part is not the model but the fence round it, a filter and a fallback that let the game use something unreliable without ever depending on it.

What the player sees

Most of the time, nothing different. Switch the feature on in Settings, download the model once, and when you stop to chat with somebody who has nothing new to say, they say something about this moment: the weather, the hour, where they are. You can reply, and they answer your reply. If your phone is slow, or the model has a bad day, you get one of the written lines instead and never know. A chapter is always the written one.

Those are whole-word patterns, so a word can be stopped without stopping every longer word that contains it. The last loop stops copying: it slides a 30-character window along the line, six characters at a time, and rejects the line if any window appears in an example it was shown.

YYears, memories and the diary

Little Hours has no ending, so it needs a way to show that time has passed. It does that by keeping a few small records that all work the same way: write the newest thing at the front, and let the oldest fall off the end. The calendar, the diary, the things left lying about in the house and the list of things to do are all that one idea, with different lengths.

What the player sees

The seasons come round, and the same spring is eventually your second one. The journal keeps a diary of the things you did, newest first. Cooking, working or picking flowers leaves a mark in the house, such as washing up by the sink, a cake on the counter or a pile of washing, and these fade or grow on their own, so a room never looks quite the same twice. The phone shows today's short list of reasons to go out, which is empty again tomorrow, and keeps a record of the notifications you swiped away.

ZZzz: sleeping, and what a new day does

Nothing in Little Hours happens at midnight. A new day starts when the player chooses it, by going to bed, and everything that depends on the date is either changed in one short function or is worked out from the date and so changes by itself. The idea worth stealing is the order of operations in that function: the world is finished first, and only then is anybody told.

What the player sees

When the clock reaches 22:00 it stops, and nothing is lost by being slow. The bed offers to sleep until morning. Pick it, and the screen shows a new morning: the day number is one higher, the crops you watered are ripe, the light is back to dawn, any washing up left in the kitchen is a day older and may be gone. If it is a new season a card announces it, and the sky may have changed. The cat has wandered off to a different room.

The rule

The clock itself is entry C; this entry begins where that one stops, with the day parked at nightfall. advance_day() does these things, in this order:

OrderWhat it does
1Adds one to state["day"]. The only number that counts time. The date, the season, the year and the weather are all read from it.
2age_traces(): anything left lying about that has outlived its life goes (entry Y).
3clock_t = 0.0 and night_held = false: the clock starts again from dawn, and runs, because nothing holds it any more.
4Every plot that is planted and watered becomes ready. An unwatered one is left alone.
5When year_day() is 1 and year() is above 1, the diary gets a line saying how long you have been here.
6day_changed.emit(day).
7_changed(): tell the screen, then save.

The one listener on the signal is Main, whose _on_new_day() opens the season card if one is due (each card is shown once, and later years have fewer of them), moves the cat to a random room at home and rebuilds the room for the new tint and weather. Several things change with the day without anything changing them. The weather is a function of the day number, so it needs no field in the save. The season is (day - 1) / DAYS_PER_SEASON wrapped round four, 5 days each. And night_held exists so that the day cannot end by itself: the bed is the way out of the night.

The idea to take away is that a signal is a promise about the state it is sent in. Anything that listens will read the world as it is at that moment, so the function that sends the signal must have finished making the world first.
