Readable interfaces

Control nodes, containers, size flags, anchors, themes and StyleBoxes – UI that survives every screen.

In this chapter

  • the two families of 2D nodes: Node2D for things in the world, Control for the interface
  • containers, and the two numbers that decide a size: the minimum, and the size flags that share the rest
  • anchors and offsets, for things that stay put in a corner or a middle on every screen
  • themes and StyleBoxes: one place that decides what everything looks like
  • text, buttons and focus, and how big a thing must be for a thumb

Two families: Node2D and Control

A tree can hold two quite different kinds of 2D things. One lives in the world and is placed by a position. The other lives on the screen and is placed by a rectangle.

A Node2D is a thing in the game's world: a character, a tree, a coin. Its place is a position (and a rotation and scale), it moves when the camera moves, and nothing about it knows how big the screen is. A Control is a thing in the interface: a label, a button, a panel. Its place is a rectangle, with a position and a size, and Godot can resize that rectangle for you when the window changes shape. Controls also have what a world object does not: a theme, a minimum size, keyboard and gamepad focus, and a way of deciding whether they or the thing underneath get a click.

Every UI node you will use – Label, Button, TextureRect, Panel, LineEdit, and all the containers – extends Control. The game's interface, like most, sits on a CanvasLayer, a node whose children are drawn over the world and are not moved by the camera. That is why a score stays in the corner while the player walks.

A rectangle, and a tap

Because a Control is a rectangle, the question "did the finger land on it?" is a question about a rectangle and a point, and Rect2 answers it:

extends Node

func _ready() -> void:
	var button := Rect2(Vector2(100, 40), Vector2(120, 64))     # position, size
	print(button.end)                                         # the far corner
	print(button.has_point(Vector2(150, 70)))
	print(button.has_point(Vector2(90, 70)))
	print(button.get_center())

Move the point to Vector2(219, 103) and then Vector2(220, 104): a rectangle includes its top-left edge and stops short of its bottom-right, so the second is outside. That is the test every button runs when you touch it. Your job is not to run it but to say where the rectangle is, and the rest of this chapter is the ways of saying that so it holds on every screen.

Mixing them up

A Control as the child of a Node2D still draws, but the container and anchor rules only apply between Controls, so a button inside a Node2D will not lay itself out and will not follow the screen. Keep the interface in its own branch – a CanvasLayer with a Control under it – and put the world in another.

Containers: let the parent do the placing

If you place every label and button with numbers, the first different screen breaks all of them. A container is a Control whose only job is to place its children, and it does the sums again every time anything changes size.

Put controls inside a container and you stop setting their positions. You describe the relationships – "these in a row, with a gap, and this one takes the spare room" – and the container works out the numbers. The ones you will use all the time:

Minimum size, and what is left

Two numbers decide how a child is sized, and they are different things.

  • The minimum size is the least a control will accept. A label's minimum is the size of its text, a button's is its text plus its margins, and you can raise it, never lower it, with custom_minimum_size. A container's own minimum is made from its children's: a row's width is the sum of the widths plus the gaps.
  • The size flags say what to do with spare room. SIZE_FILL means "stretch to the room I am given", SIZE_EXPAND means "ask for a share of the leftover space", and SIZE_EXPAND_FILL is both – the one you will write most. The others (SIZE_SHRINK_BEGIN, SIZE_SHRINK_CENTER, SIZE_SHRINK_END) keep a child small and put it at one end or in the middle.

You set them per direction: size_flags_horizontal for how a child behaves along a row, size_flags_vertical for a column. The arithmetic is short, and worth doing once by hand. A row 320 wide holds an icon (48), a label (40) and a button (64), with gaps of 8:

extends Node

func _ready() -> void:
	var width := 320
	var minimums := [48, 40, 64]
	var gap := 8
	var needed := gap * (minimums.size() - 1)
	for m in minimums:
		needed += m
	print("needed: ", needed)
	print("left over: ", width - needed)

Read it as Godot would. Every child gets its minimum, then the gaps; what is left (here 144) is shared out among the children that asked to expand, equally unless size_flags_stretch_ratio says otherwise. If the label is the only one that expands, it takes all 144 and ends up 184 wide. If nothing expands, the row has spare room at one end. Put width at 100 and run again: the leftover is negative. A container does not squeeze its children below their minimums; it asks its parent for more room, and if there is none, it overflows. That is the thing to remember when a layout breaks on a small screen: something has a minimum bigger than the room.

The trap: text that will not shrink, and text that collapses

Text is where minimum sizes surprise people. A Label is as wide as its text, so a long one will force its row wider than the screen. You can turn on autowrap_mode to let it wrap into several lines, but a wrapping label has no natural width, and Godot gives it a minimum of about one letter. In a container that sizes things to their minimum, it collapses into a thin column with one letter per line. The remedy is to give the label a width from somewhere – an expanding size flag, or a custom_minimum_size – or to leave wrapping off and trim with clip_text. The game's own label builder carries a long comment about exactly this, which you will read below.

Anchors: stay in the corner

A container places its children relative to each other. Anchors place one control relative to its parent, and they are how a button stays in a corner on a phone and on a monitor.

A Control has four anchors: left, top, right and bottom, each a number from 0 to 1 that means "this far across the parent". 0 is the parent's left or top edge, 1 is its right or bottom edge, and 0.5 is the middle. Then four offsets are pixel distances from those anchor points. So a control's left edge is parent_width * anchor_left + offset_left. When the parent changes size, the anchor points move with it and the offsets stay the same, which is the whole trick.

One edge, as a function:

extends Node

func edge(parent_len: float, anchor: float, offset: float) -> float:
	return parent_len * anchor + offset

func _ready() -> void:
	for w in [320.0, 480.0, 960.0]:
		print("parent ", w, " wide")
		print("   left-anchored,  offset 10:   ", edge(w, 0.0, 10.0))
		print("   right-anchored, offset -10:  ", edge(w, 1.0, -10.0))
		print("   centre-anchored, offset -20: ", edge(w, 0.5, -20.0))

A control with both anchors at 0 and an offset of 10 is always 10 pixels from the left, however wide the screen. With both at 1 and an offset of -10, it is always 10 pixels from the right. With both at 0.5 it is always in the middle, a fixed distance either side. And if the left anchor is 0 and the right anchor is 1, the control stretches with the parent: its left edge stays near the left and its right edge near the right, and everything between grows. That last one is how a full-width bar is made.

Drag the parent's width and height sliders and choose different presets. You almost never type the four numbers yourself: in the editor the Layout menu (the small anchor icon in the toolbar when a Control is selected) sets all eight values for a named place – Top Left, Center Bottom, Full Rect and so on – and in code set_anchors_and_offsets_preset(Control.PRESET_FULL_RECT) does the same.

Anchors, or offsets too?

There are two similar calls. set_anchors_and_offsets_preset() sets both the anchors and the offsets, so the control ends up where the preset says. set_anchors_preset() only sets the anchors and leaves the offsets as they were. On a brand new control the offsets are 0, so the two look the same for PRESET_FULL_RECT and everything stretches; but on a control that already has offsets, only the second keeps them, which can be what you want or the reason it is in the wrong place.

Anchors work whether the control is in a container or not, and so does the question of which to use. Inside a container, the container is in charge and anchors are ignored. Outside one – a corner pill, a toast at the bottom, a full-screen scrim – anchors are how you say where it goes.

Themes and StyleBoxes

A button needs a fill, a border, rounded corners, a font, colours for its label when hovered or pressed. If you set those on every button, changing the look of the game means visiting every button. A theme is the one place that says it.

A Theme is a table of look-ups

A Theme is a resource, and inside it is a table. Each entry is a name for a kind of control and a kind of thing: for example, the Button's font_color, or the Label's font_size. There are five kinds of thing – colours, constants (plain numbers like a separation), fonts, font sizes and StyleBoxes – and icons make a sixth. When a control draws, it looks up each item it needs: first an override on the node itself, then the theme of the node, then the theme of its parent, and so on up the tree, and finally the project's default theme. Set a theme on a root control and everything under it uses it.

The look-up is a short chain, and the page can run its logic. Each "node" below has its own overrides, and the answer is the first place that has the item:

extends Node

func look_up(item: String, chain: Array) -> Variant:
	for level in chain:
		if level.has(item):
			return level[item]
	return null

func _ready() -> void:
	var defaults := {"font_color": "grey", "font_size": 16}
	var root_theme := {"font_color": "ink", "font_size": 20}
	var this_label := {"font_color": "red"}
	var chain := [this_label, root_theme, defaults]      # nearest first
	print(look_up("font_color", chain))
	print(look_up("font_size", chain))
	print(look_up("separation", chain))

The label's own red wins for the colour, the root theme answers the size, and nobody has separation, so you get null, which in the real engine means "use the built-in value". Delete the red and the colour falls through to the root theme's. That is the cascade, and it is why one assignment can restyle a whole screen.

Building a theme, and overriding one thing

You can make a theme in the editor and save it as a file, or build one in code. Either way, the calls are the same:

var t := Theme.new()
t.set_color("font_color", "Label", Color("#4c3438"))
t.set_font_size("font_size", "Label", 20)
t.set_constant("separation", "VBoxContainer", 14)
t.set_stylebox("normal", "Button", normal_box)
root_control.theme = t                      # everything below now uses it

# one control, one exception
big_label.add_theme_font_size_override("font_size", 40)

Two kinds of change. Setting theme on a control affects it and all its descendants. add_theme_*_override changes one item on one node and nothing else. Keep the overrides for real exceptions: a pile of them means the theme is missing something.

StyleBox: what a background is

Backgrounds, borders and button faces are all StyleBoxes: small resources that know how to draw a rectangle. A Button has one for each state – normal, hover, pressed, disabled and focus – and a Panel has one called panel. There are three you will meet. StyleBoxFlat draws a colour with a border, rounded corners and a shadow, and needs no image. StyleBoxTexture stretches a picture, with nine-slice margins so that the corners stay sharp (below). StyleBoxEmpty draws nothing, which is how you turn a button's focus ring off. Drag the sliders and see the properties and the code:

Every StyleBox also has content margins: the space between its edge and what is inside it. That is how a button gets its padding. It is the style, not the button, that says how much room the text has, which is why a theme's buttons can all be changed at once.

Nine-slice: a box of any size from a little picture

A StyleBoxTexture with texture_margin_* set cuts its picture into nine pieces by two vertical and two horizontal lines. The four corners are drawn as they are, the four edges are stretched in one direction, and the middle in both. So a 16-pixel image becomes a panel of any size with its corners untouched. For pixel art this is the tool, since a StyleBoxFlat with rounded corners is anti-aliased, and a pixel game wants hard steps.

Text, buttons, focus and fingers

Most of an interface is words and things to press. The detail that decides whether it is pleasant is almost always size.

Label, Button and RichTextLabel

Label shows text: its text, horizontal_alignment, autowrap_mode and clip_text are what you will change, and its colour and size come from the theme. Button is a label that can be pressed; you connect its pressed signal (Chapter J), often with a lambda (Chapter F). RichTextLabel is for text that is partly styled: turn on bbcode_enabled and write the markup in the text itself.

$Note.bbcode_enabled = true
$Note.text = "You found [color=#e0808f]three[/color] shells and [wave]one[/wave] pearl."

Use a plain Label wherever you can: it is cheaper and simpler. Reach for RichTextLabel when a line needs two colours, a picture, or an effect.

Focus

A control with focus is the one that responds to the keyboard or a gamepad. focus_mode says whether a control can take it: FOCUS_ALL (by click and by keyboard), FOCUS_CLICK, or FOCUS_NONE. grab_focus() gives it to a control – call it on a menu's first button so a gamepad player can start pressing – and the arrow keys then move it to a neighbour, which you can adjust with the focus_neighbor_* properties. The focus stylebox is what is drawn on whoever has it. A game played only by touch has no use for a focus ring, and can set focus_mode to FOCUS_NONE, as the game does below; a game that is also played with a controller must keep it.

How big is big enough?

A fingertip is about a centimetre across, and does not land exactly where the player means. Platform guidelines put a comfortable minimum for something to be tapped at about 44 to 48 points, which is more than most people picture. Small controls on a phone are the commonest complaint about interfaces made on a desktop. A few rules that follow:

  • Size the target, not the picture. A 24-pixel icon can sit in a 72-pixel button: what has to be comfortable is where the thumb lands, not the glyph.
  • Leave space between targets. Two big buttons with no gap are one big button.
  • Think in proportion to the screen, since a pixel is a different size on every display. A good rule for a game that scales (Chapter P) is a minimum height that is a tenth of the view's.
extends Node

func thumb_fraction(button_h: float, view_h: float) -> float:
	return button_h / view_h

func _ready() -> void:
	for view_h in [540.0, 360.0, 720.0]:
		print("a 64 pixel button in a view ", view_h, " tall is ", "%.1f" % (thumb_fraction(64.0, view_h) * 100.0), " % of it")

In the tall view, 64 is 11.9 per cent of the height; in the short one it is 17.8, so it is roomy. Try 48 in the 540 view: 8.9 per cent, under the rule. This is the arithmetic that fixes one number in your game for good, the way the game fixes its button height.

The root Control of the HUD has root.theme = UI.theme() and everything beneath inherits it, the cascade you ran. The boxes are not StyleBoxFlat: pixel_box draws a tiny nine-slice picture with hard stepped corners and a solid shadow, and wraps it in a StyleBoxTexture, for the reason in the "Nine-slice" section. UI.gd · lines 498–513

## A single-line label. Deliberately does NOT autowrap: an autowrapping Label
## reports a ~1-character minimum width, so any container that sizes to its
## minimum (an HBox column without EXPAND_FILL, say) squeezes it to one glyph
## per line — which is how the HUD's clock once grew ~20 lines tall and pushed
## the status bar over most of the screen. Use `para()` for real paragraphs,
## where the width is bounded by a panel and wrapping is what you want.
static func label(text: String, size: int = SZ_BODY, color: Color = NO_COLOR, bold: bool = false) -> Label:
	var l := Label.new()
	l.text = text
	l.add_theme_font_size_override("font_size", size)
	l.add_theme_color_override("font_color", _or_default(color, INK))
	var f := font(bold)
	if f:
		l.add_theme_font_override("font", f)
	l.autowrap_mode = TextServer.AUTOWRAP_OFF
	return l

Two small builders, and a lot of the chapter. button sets focus_mode to FOCUS_NONE – this is a touch game, so no focus ring – and gives it custom_minimum_size = Vector2(0, BUTTON_H), a minimum height with the width left to the container: a thumb target, not a glyph. BUTTON_H is 64, and its comment says why: it is about 12 per cent of the 540-pixel view when held sideways, and "the suite holds it to at least a tenth on every phone it knows". The rule you computed is a test in the game. label is the trap from the containers section, with a comment to match: it deliberately does not wrap, because a wrapping label has a one-letter minimum and a container that sizes to its content squeezes it to a column; the HUD's clock once did exactly that, grew about twenty lines tall and pushed the status bar over the screen. Wrapping text is a separate builder, para, to be used only where a panel bounds the width.

The top strip of the screen is the tutorial's row. A MarginContainer anchored with PRESET_TOP_WIDE stretches across the top; its margins are theme constants that keep 28 pixels off the sides. Inside is a HBoxContainer with the status pills in a column on the left, then a gap, an empty Control with SIZE_EXPAND_FILL, which takes all the room that is left and so pushes the round buttons to the far right. It is your step 3 at work: one child asks for the leftover and the rest are as small as they can be, on a phone of any width. (The comment, "justify-content: space-between", names the same idea in web layout.)

The toast, the little message that rises near the bottom, is anchoring. It is not in a container. PRESET_CENTER_BOTTOM puts it at the bottom, then the anchors are set by hand to 0.5 left and right and 1.0 top and bottom, so the whole box hangs from the bottom-middle point of the screen; and the offsets then say how far from that point each edge is (-330 to 330 across, -190 to -110 up). It stays at the bottom-middle on a screen of any size, 660 wide and 80 tall. It is your anchored(), with 0.5 and 1.0 for the anchors. The little drawer, phone and cards are built the same way, in Overlays; the _row you met in Chapter F is one row of a card, its label taking SIZE_EXPAND_FILL and its button a minimum of 190 by 62.

What to remember

  • Node2D is for the world and is placed by position. Control is for the interface and is placed by a rectangle. Keep the interface in its own branch, on a CanvasLayer.
  • Containers place their children for you. Each child gets its minimum size, then the leftover is shared among the children with SIZE_EXPAND, by size_flags_stretch_ratio. Nobody shrinks below their minimum.
  • A wrapping Label has no natural width. Give it one from a container or custom_minimum_size, or the container collapses it.
  • Anchors (0 to 1) say what fraction of the parent; offsets are pixels from there. Both anchors at 1 sticks to the right; 0 and 1 stretches; 0.5 stays central.
  • A theme is a table of colours, constants, fonts, sizes and StyleBoxes that cascades down the tree. add_theme_*_override is for one-offs. A nine-slice StyleBoxTexture keeps pixel corners crisp at any size.
  • Size the target, not the picture: make touch targets a tenth of the screen's height, or about 44 to 48 points, and turn focus_mode off only if nobody plays with a keyboard or a gamepad.
