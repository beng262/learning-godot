Quiet and loud: sound

AudioStreamPlayer, buses, decibels, music versus effects, and making sound from nothing.

In this chapter

  • the three parts of playing a sound: the stream (the sound), the player (what plays it) and the bus (where it goes)
  • decibels: why volume is a negative number, and how to turn a 0 to 1 slider into one
  • what a sound is made of – samples, frequency, a waveform – and a note from a semitone
  • music against effects: loops, a pool of players, and keeping sounds from cutting each other off
  • making a sound from nothing, as AudioStreamWAV from a list of numbers

Streams, players and buses

To hear something in Godot you need three things, and each one is a separate piece. Knowing which is which is most of the chapter.

The stream is the sound

An AudioStream is a sound held as data: a recording, a loop, or something built in code. It is a resource, like a texture, not a node. It does not play itself, and many players can play the same one. The kinds you will meet: AudioStreamWAV, which is uncompressed and starts instantly, so it is the choice for short effects; AudioStreamOggVorbis and AudioStreamMP3, which are compressed and suit long things such as music. You drop a .wav, .ogg or .mp3 file into the project and Godot imports it; the Import dock has a Loop switch for a sound that should go round and round.

The player plays it

A player is a node. AudioStreamPlayer plays a sound the same everywhere: music, interface clicks, a jingle. AudioStreamPlayer2D gives the sound a place in the 2D world, so it gets quieter with distance and pans from side to side as it moves relative to the listener. (There is a 3D one as well.) The properties you will actually set:

Property or functionWhat it does
streamwhich sound this player has
play(), stop()start it (from the beginning) or stop it
playingtrue while it is sounding
volume_dbloudness, in decibels (next section)
pitch_scalespeed and pitch together: 2.0 is an octave up, 0.5 an octave down
autoplaystart by itself when the node is ready
busthe name of the bus it plays into
finisheda signal (Chapter J), sent when the sound ends
 
extends Node

@onready var click: AudioStreamPlayer = $Click     # a child with its stream set in the Inspector

func _on_button_pressed() -> void:
	click.play()

The first thing that trips everyone: one player plays one sound at a time. Call play() on a player that is still sounding and it starts again from the beginning, cutting itself off. Ten coins collected in a second on one player sound like one broken coin. The fix is a handful of players taken in turn, and you will write the idea below.

The bus is where it goes

Players do not talk to the speakers. Each sends its sound down a bus, a channel that can have its own volume, mute, and effects (reverb, an equaliser...), and the buses feed into the one called Master, which goes to the speakers. You build them in the Audio panel at the bottom of the editor, usually a Music bus and an SFX bus, and each player says which with its bus property. Then one slider can turn all the music down without touching the effects:

func set_music_volume(db: float) -> void:
	var bus := AudioServer.get_bus_index("Music")
	AudioServer.set_bus_volume_db(bus, db)

func mute_everything(on: bool) -> void:
	AudioServer.set_bus_mute(AudioServer.get_bus_index("Master"), on)

Players, buses and the AudioServer need the engine, so these are listings rather than runnables: the page has no speakers and no sound. What the page can do is the arithmetic, and that is where the rest of the chapter lives.

Decibels: loudness is logarithmic

Every volume in Godot is in decibels, and 0 is the loudest it will normally go. Quieter is negative. That looks odd until you know why the ear works this way.

Your ear does not hear sound pressure; it hears ratios. A sound with half the pressure of another does not seem half as loud – it seems a little quieter. So engineers measure in decibels (dB), a scale built on ratios. The conversion from a plain amount (1.0 is "unchanged", 0.5 is "half as big", 2.0 is "twice as big") is:

db = 20 × log10(amount) and back again: amount = 10 ^ (db / 20)

Godot has these built in as linear_to_db() and db_to_linear(). The interpreter in this book does not, so here is how to write them yourself – and how they are made:

extends Node

func to_db(amount: float) -> float:
	return 20.0 * log(amount) / log(10.0)       # log() is the natural log, so divide by log(10)

func from_db(db: float) -> float:
	return pow(10.0, db / 20.0)

func _ready() -> void:
	for a in [1.0, 0.5, 0.25, 0.1, 2.0]:
		print("amount %.2f is %.1f dB" % [a, to_db(a)])
	print("-6 dB is an amount of %.3f" % from_db(-6.0))
	print("-20 dB is an amount of %.3f" % from_db(-20.0))

Read the table you just printed, because it is worth knowing by heart. 0 dB is "unchanged". -6 dB is about half. -20 dB is a tenth, and -40 dB a hundredth, near enough silence. Every +6 dB doubles it. Add a few more amounts to the list, 0.01 and 4.0, and see where they fall. A sound can go above 0 dB; the sum of loud sounds can then clip, where the numbers reach past what the speaker can show and it distorts. A good mix keeps its peaks at or under 0.

Why not use the slider's number as it is?

A volume slider that runs from 0 to 1 is easy to understand, and the first idea is to set volume_db to some multiple of it. Try it: what does a slider at one half do?

extends Node

func to_db(amount: float) -> float:
	return 20.0 * log(amount) / log(10.0)

func wrong(level: float) -> float:
	return -80.0 + level * 80.0       # linear in dB: half the slider is -40 dB

func right(level: float) -> float:
	if level <= 0.001:
		return -80.0                  # silence: log(0) has no answer
	return to_db(level)

func _ready() -> void:
	for level in [1.0, 0.75, 0.5, 0.25, 0.0]:
		print("slider %.2f: wrong %.1f dB, right %.1f dB" % [level, wrong(level), right(level)])

With the first mapping the slider is nearly silent at half-way (-40 dB, a hundredth) and then rushes up to full over the last part: all of the useful range is crammed into the top. With the second, half the slider is -6 dB, which is half as loud, as it should be. The if at the start is not decoration: the log of 0 is minus infinity, which no mixer wants, so silence is written as a very quiet -80 dB. That function is the game's own, as you will see.

Pick a loudness once

Do not give every effect the same volume_db and then fix the balance in the speakers. Choose each sound's own level when you make it – a click quieter than a chime, a bark between them – and let the player's slider scale all of them together. In dB scaling everything together is an addition, which is the reason the relative mix survives.

What a sound is made of

A sound on a computer is a long list of numbers between -1 and 1: where the speaker cone should be, thousands of times a second. Each number is a sample. Play the list quickly and you hear it.

Samples, rate and frequency

The number of samples every second is the sample rate: 44100 for a CD, 22050 in Little Hours. A sound that repeats a hundred times a second has a frequency of 100 hertz (Hz), and we hear frequency as pitch. Low is a rumble, a few hundred hertz is a voice, a few thousand is a bell. If a wave repeats every RATE / f samples, a sine wave is written down by feeding its position through sin:

extends Node

const RATE := 8000        # samples a second (22050 in the game, 44100 on a CD)

func sine_cycle(f: float) -> Array:
	var n := int(RATE / f)               # samples in one cycle
	var out := []
	for i in n:
		out.append(sin(TAU * i / n))     # TAU is a full turn: 2 pi
	return out

func _ready() -> void:
	var c := sine_cycle(1000.0)          # eight samples a cycle
	for v in c:
		print("%6.2f  %s" % [v, "#".repeat(int(round((v + 1.0) * 10.0)))])

You printed the shape of the wave sideways. The values rise to 1, fall through 0 to -1 and come round again, and the bar is longest at the top of the wave. Change 1000 to 500 and the same cycle is twice as long, with twice as many samples in it: slower, and so a lower note.

Four shapes

The sine is the purest tone. The other shapes are all different ways to go up and down, and each has a character: a square wave is buzzy, a triangle is soft, a saw is bright and rough. They are one-line functions of how far through the cycle you are:

extends Node

func sine(t: float) -> float:
	return sin(TAU * t)

func square(t: float) -> float:
	return 1.0 if fposmod(t, 1.0) < 0.5 else -1.0

func triangle(t: float) -> float:
	return asin(sin(TAU * t)) * (2.0 / PI)

func saw(t: float) -> float:
	return fposmod(t, 1.0) * 2.0 - 1.0

func _ready() -> void:
	for i in 9:
		var t := i / 8.0                      # 0.0 to 1.0, one whole cycle
		print("t=%.3f  sine %5.2f  square %5.2f  triangle %5.2f  saw %5.2f" % [t, sine(t), square(t), triangle(t), saw(t)])

t is counted in whole cycles, not in samples or seconds: 0.25 is a quarter of the way round. fposmod(t, 1.0) wraps it into 0 to 1 whatever t is, so a wave can keep going for ever. Compare the columns at 0.25, where the sine peaks, and at 0.5, where the square flips. (The game's speech uses these same four shapes and a few more: V: Voices and sound.)

An envelope

A wave that starts at full strength on the first sample, and stops dead on the last, clicks: the speaker is thrown from silence to the wave in one jump. Real sounds start and end gently. An envelope is a number from 0 to 1 that you multiply every sample by, to shape how the sound's loudness changes over its life: a short attack to rise, then a decay to fall away.

extends Node

func envelope(x: float) -> float:        # x is how far through the sound, 0.0 to 1.0
	return minf(1.0, x / 0.15) * pow(1.0 - x, 2.2)

func _ready() -> void:
	for i in 11:
		var x := i / 10.0
		print("%.1f  %s" % [x, "#".repeat(int(envelope(x) * 40.0))])

The bar rises in the first sixth of the sound (that is the x / 0.15) and falls away to nothing at the end (the pow(1.0 - x, 2.2), which is exactly 0 at x = 1). Change the 0.15 to 0.5 for a slow swell, or the 2.2 to 6.0 for a short, sharp "pluck". Those two numbers are the difference between a pad and a pop. This is the game's own speech envelope, nearly line for line.

Notes: a semitone is a ratio

Pitch is not added; it is multiplied. Going up an octave doubles the frequency. A piano's octave is split into twelve equal steps called semitones, so each one multiplies the frequency by the same amount: the twelfth root of 2, about 1.0595. Twelve of them make 2. Everything about tuning follows, including the formula for any note from its MIDI number (60 is middle C, 69 is the A above, at 440 Hz):

extends Node

func note_freq(midi: int) -> float:
	return 440.0 * pow(2.0, float(midi - 69) / 12.0)

func _ready() -> void:
	print("A4:  %.2f Hz" % note_freq(69))
	print("C4:  %.2f Hz" % note_freq(60))
	print("A5:  %.2f Hz" % note_freq(81))
	print("a fifth above A4: %.2f Hz" % note_freq(76))

Look at the conversion float(midi - 69) / 12.0. Chapter D's trap is waiting: with two ints, (midi - 69) / 12 would be a whole number, and every note between two octaves would come out as the one below. And the very same ratio is what pitch_scale is. A player with pitch_scale = 2.0 plays its stream an octave higher, and pow(2.0, semitones / 12.0) is how you set it to any number of semitones, which lets one recording of a note become every note on a keyboard.

Music, effects, and sound from nothing

Two kinds of sound need two kinds of handling, and once you have a list of samples, you can make either without a single audio file.

Effects need a pool; music needs a loop

An effect is short, happens often, and must not cut itself off. Keep several players in an array, and for each new sound use the next one that is not busy. If all are busy, take the oldest:

const VOICES := 8
var _players: Array[AudioStreamPlayer] = []
var _next := 0

func _ready() -> void:
	for i in VOICES:
		var p := AudioStreamPlayer.new()
		add_child(p)
		_players.append(p)

func play(stream: AudioStream, volume_db: float = -9.0) -> void:
	for i in VOICES:
		var p := _players[(_next + i) % VOICES]
		if not p.playing:
			_next = (_next + i + 1) % VOICES
			p.stream = stream
			p.volume_db = volume_db
			p.play()
			return
	var oldest := _players[_next]       # everyone is busy: cut in on the oldest
	_next = (_next + 1) % VOICES
	oldest.stream = stream
	oldest.volume_db = volume_db
	oldest.play()

The % from Chapter E turns a counter into a position that wraps round the array: round-robin, so that a rapid burst uses every player in turn. Music is the opposite: one player, one long stream, set to loop so that it never ends, and with its own volume slider and its own off switch, so that someone who wants the effects and not the tune can have that. Put music and effects on different buses (or at least different players), and give each its own level; nobody wants the pad as loud as the door.

A last trick for effects that happen a lot: change pitch_scale a little each time, a few per cent either way. The same footstep, forty times at exactly the same pitch, is a machine; with a hair of variation it is a person walking.

An AudioStreamWAV from numbers

Everything above takes a stream as given. But a stream is only data, and AudioStreamWAV lets you fill one yourself. Four things to set: the format (16 bits a sample is the usual), the mix_rate (the sample rate), whether it is stereo, and the data, a PackedByteArray of the bytes. To turn a sample between -1.0 and 1.0 into the whole number a 16-bit sound is stored as, multiply by a number a little under 32767 and write it as two bytes. Here is that conversion, which the page can run:

extends Node

func to_s16(v: float) -> int:
	return int(clampf(v, -1.0, 1.0) * 31000.0)       # a little under 32767, to leave headroom

func _ready() -> void:
	for v in [0.0, 0.5, 1.0, -1.0, 1.7, -0.25]:
		print("%5.2f  becomes  %6d" % [v, to_s16(v)])

The clampf is there because a sample outside the range would wrap round to the opposite end and make a loud crack instead of a clipped sound. Notice that 1.7 and 1.0 end in the same place. The engine pieces – PackedFloat32Array for the samples, encode_s16 to write them into bytes and the AudioStreamWAV itself – belong to the engine and not to this page, so you will meet them in the game's code below rather than here. To loop a stream, set loop_mode to AudioStreamWAV.LOOP_FORWARD and give it a loop_end.

Build it live

The page cannot play a sound, but it can draw one. You will build the pieces of a tone – a wave, its shape, its envelope, its loudness, its pitch – and watch the waveform change as each arrives. A speaker would draw the same picture, and the arithmetic is the arithmetic the game does.

In Little Hours

There is not one audio file in Little Hours. Every effect, the music, each person's speaking voice and every note of the piano is a list of numbers made in code, and played through the nodes you have just met. The recipes are in Sfx, and the sound as the player knows it is V: Voices and sound.

A slider, turned into decibels

Sfx.gd · lines 114–119

## Linear 0..1 → dB, relative to `base_db` at full. Silence is -80dB (inaudible)
## rather than -inf, which some platforms dislike.
func _level_to_db(level: float, base_db: float) -> float:
	if level <= 0.001:
		return -80.0
	return base_db + linear_to_db(level)

func apply_settings() -> void:

	var st: Dictionary = state["settings"]
	Sfx.enabled = bool(st.get("sfx", true))
	Sfx.set_sfx_volume(float(st.get("sfx_vol", 0.8)))
	Sfx.set_music_volume(float(st.get("music_vol", 0.7)))

	Sfx.voices_enabled = bool(st.get("voices", true))

The first is the function you wrote in the decibels section, one for one. A level between 0 and 1 becomes base_db + linear_to_db(level): the base_db is each sound's own loudness, so the slider is added on top and the mix is kept. Its comment gives the reason for the early return: "Silence is -80dB (inaudible) rather than -inf, which some platforms dislike." The second is where the settings meet the sound. Whenever a volume or switch changes, Game reads the saved settings and pushes each one into Sfx: effects on or off, the two levels, music on or off, voices on or off. set_volume in Game is what the sliders call: it clamps the number to 0..1, stores it, calls apply_settings and saves. Settings are in S: Saving. Notice what the game does not have: no Music or SFX bus. It keeps two levels and trims each player's volume_db itself, and the one bus it touches is Master, which it mutes when the game is under test so that the checks do not play sounds from the speakers of whoever is at the keyboard.

A sound from nothing

Sfx.gd · lines 392–403

## A sine that glides from f0 to f1 with an exponential fade.
func _sweep(f0: float, f1: float, dur: float, curve: float = 4.0) -> PackedFloat32Array:
	var n := _len(dur)
	var out := PackedFloat32Array()
	out.resize(n)
	var phase := 0.0
	for i in range(n):
		var t := float(i) / float(n)
		var f: float = lerpf(f0, f1, t)
		phase += TAU * f / float(RATE)
		out[i] = sin(phase) * pow(1.0 - t, curve)
	return out

Sfx.gd · lines 367–387

func _wav(samples: PackedFloat32Array, loop: bool = false) -> AudioStreamWAV:
	var w := AudioStreamWAV.new()
	w.format = AudioStreamWAV.FORMAT_16_BITS
	w.mix_rate = RATE
	w.stereo = false
	var bytes := PackedByteArray()
	bytes.resize(samples.size() * 2)
	for i in range(samples.size()):
		var v: float = clampf(samples[i], -1.0, 1.0)
		bytes.encode_s16(i * 2, int(v * 31000.0))
	w.data = bytes
	if loop:
		w.loop_mode = AudioStreamWAV.LOOP_FORWARD
		w.loop_begin = 0
		# The LAST frame, not one past it. loop_end is a frame index, and the
		# mixer reads pos + 1 to interpolate, so an end equal to the frame count
		# sends it off the end of the buffer — a SIGSEGV in the AudioTrack
		# thread a few seconds after launch, once the looping music comes round.
		# It killed the app on Android and never once showed up on desktop.
		w.loop_end = maxi(0, samples.size() - 2)
	return w

_sweep is the building block of the click, pop, bark and meow: a sine that slides from one frequency to another while it fades away. t is how far through it is, the frequency is a lerpf between the two ends, and the fade is the envelope idea from the chapter. The phase is accumulated sample by sample (phase += TAU * f / RATE) rather than computed from the time, so that a frequency that changes does not make the wave jump. _wav turns the list into a stream: it sets the format and rate, clamps and scales each sample to 16 bits with encode_s16, and, for the music, sets the loop. Read its comment on loop_end: the loop point is the last frame, because one past it read beyond the buffer and crashed the Android build a few seconds in. It is the sort of fact only a real project teaches.

The effects are then played the way the chapter described. play(name, volume_db) keeps VOICES (8) AudioStreamPlayer nodes and takes them round-robin with %, taking the oldest when all are busy, and the speech blips and the piano notes have their own pools, so that forty blips in a sentence never cut off a chime. The notes use pitch_scale = pow(2.0, semitones / 12.0), exactly the ratio from the chapter: a few notes are made in advance, and the ones between are those played a little faster or slower. The sound of a person is the wave match you wrote ("sine", "tri", "square", "pulse", "saw" and "breath") driven by a few numbers per person in Voices.

One thing in the neighbourhood is silent. Ambient, which fills the outdoors with gulls, birds and butterflies, is entirely drawn: it makes no sound, and the room's sound does not change when they fly past.

What to remember

  • A stream is the sound, a player plays it, a bus carries it to the speakers. One player plays one sound at a time, so keep a small pool and take them in turn.
  • Volume is in decibels: 0 is unchanged, -6 is about half, -20 a tenth. Turn a 0 to 1 slider into dB with linear_to_db and handle zero separately, as -80.
  • Music is one looping stream with its own level and switch; effects are short, frequent and need the pool. Vary pitch_scale a little to keep repeated sounds alive.
  • A sound is a list of samples between -1 and 1, played at a sample rate. Frequency is pitch; the wave's shape is its character; an envelope stops it clicking.
  • A semitone multiplies frequency by 2 to the power of 1/12. The same ratio is pitch_scale, and with float() it is also a note from a number.
  • AudioStreamWAV is made from numbers: format, mix_rate, 16-bit data, and a loop. That is how a game can have no audio files at all.
