Movement and collision

CharacterBody2D and move_and_slide, Area2D, collision layers – and doing it by hand, the way Tiny Home does.

In this chapter

  • Godot's own way to move a character: CharacterBody2D, velocity and move_and_slide()
  • noticing without blocking: Area2D, body_entered, and the CollisionShape2D every one of them needs
  • collision layers and masks: who can touch whom
  • the same job by hand, with rectangles and circles – which is how Little Hours does it
  • walking to a point without overshooting, and sliding along a wall instead of sticking to it

A body that moves

Chapter L put things in places. This one moves them and stops them going through each other. Godot has a physics engine for that, and for a character you drive yourself the node to reach for is CharacterBody2D.

Godot has three kinds of solid body, and the difference is who moves them:

NodeWho moves itUse it for
StaticBody2DNobodywalls, floors, furniture
CharacterBody2DYour codethe player, enemies, anything that walks
RigidBody2DThe physics engine: gravity, pushes, bouncescrates, balls, things that tumble

A body has no shape of its own. Each one needs a CollisionShape2D child holding a shape – a rectangle, a circle, a capsule – and that shape, not the picture, is what bumps into things. The next section comes back to it.

velocity, and move_and_slide()

A CharacterBody2D has a property called velocity: a Vector2 in pixels a second. You say how fast you would like to be going, call move_and_slide(), and Godot moves the body that far for one tick – unless something solid is in the way, in which case it stops at the surface and slides along it with whatever movement is left. This is a whole top-down character:

extends CharacterBody2D

const SPEED := 200.0

func _physics_process(_delta: float) -> void:   # <1>
	var dir := Input.get_vector("ui_left", "ui_right", "ui_up", "ui_down")
	velocity = dir * SPEED                       # <2>
	move_and_slide()                             # <3>
  1. Physics code belongs in _physics_process, which runs at a fixed rate – 60 times a second unless you change it – so collisions behave the same on every machine (Chapter I).
  2. Pixels a second, with no delta. move_and_slide() multiplies by the tick's length itself; multiply again and the character crawls.
  3. No arguments, in Godot 4. It reads velocity, moves, and writes back what is left of it after any bump. (In Godot 3 you passed the velocity in and got one back; a tutorial that writes velocity = move_and_slide(velocity) is from then.)

For a game seen from above, set the body's Motion Mode to Floating in the Inspector. The default, Grounded, is for platformers: it treats one direction as "down" and surfaces as floors, walls and ceilings.

The physics cannot run in this page, but the idea of a velocity can. Under everything, movement is one line – position += velocity * delta – done every tick:

extends Node

func _ready() -> void:
	var pos := Vector2(0, 100)
	var velocity := Vector2(120, 0)   # pixels a SECOND, to the right
	var delta := 0.25                 # a very slow tick, to keep the numbers readable
	for tick in 4:
		pos += velocity * delta
		print("after tick ", tick + 1, ": ", pos)

Four quarter-second ticks, 120 pixels. Change delta to 0.125 and the loop to 8: twice as many steps, the same place at the end. That is what multiplying by delta buys you.

Gravity, jumping and is_on_floor()

In a platformer the velocity is not replaced every tick, it is kept: gravity adds a little downward speed each tick, and a jump is one sudden upward speed that gravity then eats away. This is close to the script Godot writes for you when you attach a new script to a CharacterBody2D:

extends CharacterBody2D

const SPEED := 300.0
const JUMP_VELOCITY := -400.0      # negative: y points down, so up is minus

func _physics_process(delta: float) -> void:
	if not is_on_floor():
		velocity += get_gravity() * delta        # <1>

	if Input.is_action_just_pressed("ui_accept") and is_on_floor():   # <2>
		velocity.y = JUMP_VELOCITY

	var direction := Input.get_axis("ui_left", "ui_right")
	if direction:
		velocity.x = direction * SPEED
	else:
		velocity.x = move_toward(velocity.x, 0, SPEED)   # <3>

	move_and_slide()
  1. Gravity is an acceleration – speed gained per second – so here delta is needed. get_gravity() reads the project's gravity setting, 980 pixels a second every second by default.
  2. is_on_floor() is true when the last move_and_slide() ended with the body resting on something below it. Without the check you could jump again in mid-air. Its siblings are is_on_wall() and is_on_ceiling().
  3. move_toward(from, to, step) walks a number towards another without passing it. You will use it a great deal later in this chapter.

The arc that code produces is plain arithmetic, so here it is by hand, with a floor at y = 300:

extends Node

const GRAVITY := 980.0
const JUMP_VELOCITY := -400.0
const FLOOR_Y := 300.0

func _ready() -> void:
	var y := FLOOR_Y
	var vy := JUMP_VELOCITY     # the tick the jump key went down
	var delta := 0.1
	var t := 0.0
	while true:
		vy += GRAVITY * delta
		y = minf(y + vy * delta, FLOOR_Y)
		t += delta
		print("%.1f s   %3d px off the floor   vy %d" % [t, int(FLOOR_Y - y), int(vy)])
		if y >= FLOOR_Y:
			break

Watch vy climb from −302 through zero at the top of the jump and into positive numbers on the way down. Make JUMP_VELOCITY -600.0 for a higher jump, or halve GRAVITY for the moon.

What did I hit?

After move_and_slide() the body remembers every bump of that tick. get_slide_collision_count() says how many there were and get_slide_collision(i) returns each one, with get_collider() for the thing that was hit and get_normal() for the direction the surface faces. That is how a character knows it walked into a spike and not a wall.

Noticing without blocking

A coin should not stop you. A doorway, a patch of tall grass, the reach of an enemy's sight: these need to know something is inside them, not to push it back. That is an Area2D.

An Area2D is a region with signals (Chapter J). When a body crosses into it, it emits body_entered, passing the body; when the body leaves, body_exited. There is a matching pair for other areas, area_entered and area_exited. A coin is an area, a shape, a sprite and a few lines:

extends Area2D

signal collected

func _ready() -> void:
	body_entered.connect(_on_body_entered)

func _on_body_entered(body: Node2D) -> void:
	if body.is_in_group("player"):     # groups: Chapter C
		collected.emit()
		queue_free()

The signal fires once, at the moment of crossing, not on every tick the body is inside. If you need to ask at some other moment, get_overlapping_bodies() returns everything inside it now.

CollisionShape2D: the part people forget

Neither a body nor an area does anything until it has a shape. In the editor you add a CollisionShape2D as a child, and in its Inspector choose a Shape – RectangleShape2D, CircleShape2D, CapsuleShape2D – then drag its handles in the viewport. The same thing in code shows what the two pieces are:

var circle := CircleShape2D.new()      # the shape: a resource, only numbers
circle.radius = 12.0

var shape_node := CollisionShape2D.new()   # the node that puts it in the world
shape_node.shape = circle
add_child(shape_node)

Godot warns you with a yellow triangle in the Scene dock when a body or area has no shape. Read it: "my coin does nothing" is nearly always this. And make shapes smaller than the picture – a character's shape is usually only its feet and legs, so that it can stand close to things and its head can overlap what is behind it.

An area's signals are less mysterious once you have written them yourself. Each tick, ask "is it inside?", and compare with the answer from the tick before. Only a change is news:

extends Node

signal entered
signal exited

var zone := Rect2(100, 0, 60, 40)
var inside := false

func check(p: Vector2) -> void:
	var now := zone.has_point(p)
	if now and not inside:
		entered.emit()
	elif inside and not now:
		exited.emit()
	inside = now

func _ready() -> void:
	entered.connect(func(): print("   -> entered"))
	exited.connect(func(): print("   -> exited"))
	for x in range(60, 221, 20):
		print("walker at x = ", x)
		check(Vector2(x, 20))

Nine positions, two announcements. Delete the line inside = now and run it again: without a memory of last tick, "entered" fires on every tick inside, which is the bug behind coins that are collected four times.

Who can touch whom

The player should hit walls and pick up coins. Enemies should hit walls and ignore coins. Bullets should hit enemies and pass through the player who fired them. Layers and masks are how you say so without a line of code.

Every body and area has two sets of 32 tick-boxes in the Inspector, under Collision:

  • Layer – collision_layer – is what this object is: "I am in these layers."
  • Mask – collision_mask – is what this object looks for: "I notice things in these layers."

One object notices another when its mask names a layer the other is in. That is the whole rule. Give the layers names in Project Settings ▸ Layer Names ▸ 2D Physics and a small game might read:

ObjectLayer (is)Mask (looks for)
Player, a CharacterBody2D1 player2 walls
Wall, a StaticBody2D2 wallsnone
Enemy, a CharacterBody2D4 enemies2 walls
Coin, an Area2D3 coins1 player

The coin looks for the player, so its body_entered fires for the player and never for an enemy. The wall looks for nothing: it only has to be found.

In code, set_collision_layer_value(3, true) and set_collision_mask_value(1, true) tick one box each, by number. The two properties themselves are single integers with one bit per box, so layer 1 is the value 1, layer 2 is 2, layer 3 is 4, layer 4 is 8. You rarely need the raw numbers, but they explain the odd-looking values you will see in a .tscn file:

extends Node

const WALLS := 2      # layer numbers, as named in Project Settings
const COINS := 3

## The value of one layer's bit.
func bit(layer_number: int) -> int:
	return 1 << (layer_number - 1)

func _ready() -> void:
	print(bit(1), " ", bit(2), " ", bit(3), " ", bit(4))

	var coin_layer := bit(COINS)
	var player_mask := bit(WALLS) | bit(COINS)     # | joins bits together
	var enemy_mask := bit(WALLS)
	print("the player's mask, as a number: ", player_mask)
	print("player notices the coin: ", (player_mask & coin_layer) != 0)   # & keeps the shared bits
	print("enemy notices the coin:  ", (enemy_mask & coin_layer) != 0)

Add | bit(COINS) to the enemy's mask and it starts noticing coins.

The trap: everything starts on layer 1, looking at layer 1

A new body or area is in layer 1 with a mask of 1, so at first everything notices everything and the settings seem not to matter. Then you move the walls to layer 2 "to be tidy", forget the player's mask, and the player falls through the floor. When something passes through something else, read the pair the right way round: does the mover's mask name the other's layer?

By hand: rectangles

A physics engine is the right tool for crates that tumble. A character walking round a room of furniture needs much less: a list of rectangles and three questions. Writing it yourself is a page of code you understand completely – and it is the half of this chapter the page can run.

The rectangles are Rect2s: a position (the top-left corner) and a size, with end for the opposite corner. They are axis-aligned – never rotated – which is what makes the questions cheap. Two of them come built in:

extends Node

func _ready() -> void:
	var sofa := Rect2(100, 60, 80, 40)         # x, y, width, height
	print(sofa.position, " to ", sofa.end)

	print(sofa.has_point(Vector2(120, 70)))     # is this point inside? a tap on the sofa
	print(sofa.has_point(Vector2(90, 70)))      # a tap beside it

	var you := Rect2(170, 90, 16, 16)
	print(sofa.intersects(you))                 # do two rectangles overlap? yes, at the corner
	you = Rect2(180, 90, 16, 16)
	print(sofa.intersects(you))                 # edges only touching: that does not count

has_point() is the question for taps and clicks. intersects() is the question for bodies, and the last line matters: two rectangles that only share an edge do not intersect, so a character can stand flush against a wall without being "inside" it. Move you to x = 179 and it is.

One axis at a time

Now the walker. Before each step, ask whether the body would overlap something at the place it is about to be. The obvious rule – if the new place is blocked, do not go – has a flaw you can feel in any game that gets it wrong: walk diagonally into a wall and you stop dead, as if the wall were glue. The step was half "towards the wall" and half "along it", and refusing all of it throws the good half away.

The cure is to take the step in two parts. Try the horizontal part and keep it if it is clear; then try the vertical part from wherever that left you. A wall refuses one part and the other carries on: you slide.

extends Node

const SIZE := Vector2(16, 16)          # the walker's body
var wall := Rect2(100, 0, 20, 200)

func body_at(p: Vector2) -> Rect2:
	return Rect2(p - SIZE / 2, SIZE)    # a box centred on p

func blocked(p: Vector2) -> bool:
	return wall.intersects(body_at(p))

func move_all_at_once(p: Vector2, step: Vector2) -> Vector2:
	if blocked(p + step):
		return p
	return p + step

func move_by_axis(p: Vector2, step: Vector2) -> Vector2:
	if not blocked(Vector2(p.x + step.x, p.y)):
		p.x += step.x
	if not blocked(Vector2(p.x, p.y + step.y)):
		p.y += step.y
	return p

func _ready() -> void:
	var a := Vector2(80, 50)
	var b := Vector2(80, 50)
	var step := Vector2(6, 6)           # down and to the right, into the wall
	for frame in 5:
		a = move_all_at_once(a, step)
		b = move_by_axis(b, step)
		print("all at once: ", a, "     by axis: ", b)

Both reach the wall on the second frame. After that the left column never changes, while the right one keeps its x and goes on down: 68, 74, 80. Change step to Vector2(6, -6) and it slides up instead. This is, in miniature, what move_and_slide() is named after.

The trap: steps bigger than walls

This check looks only at where the body lands. A step longer than a wall is thick lands on the far side and the wall is never seen – the classic way for a fast bullet to pass through a thin door. For a walker at a few pixels a frame it cannot happen; for anything fast, shorten the step or make the walls thicker than the fastest step.

Walking to a point, and circles

Tap the floor and the character walks there. Two small ideas make that work: arriving without overshooting, and a rounder shape than a box.

move_toward: arrive, do not overshoot

The first walker everyone writes adds direction * speed * delta every frame and checks whether it has arrived. It never quite has. The last step carries it past the target, the next brings it back past it the other way, and it stands there shivering.

extends Node

const SPEED := 90.0

func _ready() -> void:
	var target := 100.0
	var a := 60.0      # walks by adding a step in the right direction
	var b := 60.0      # walks with move_toward
	for frame in 7:
		a += signf(target - a) * SPEED * 0.1
		b = move_toward(b, target, SPEED * 0.1)
		print("adding: ", a, "     move_toward: ", b)

The left column goes 96, 105, 96, 105 for ever. move_toward(from, to, step) takes the same step unless that would pass the target, in which case it lands exactly on it, and then stays. Vector2 has the same thing as a method: pos = pos.move_toward(target, SPEED * delta) is a complete walk-to-a-point, and pos == target is a safe test for "arrived".

It is also how to give movement weight. Move the velocity towards the velocity you want, instead of setting it outright, and the character takes a moment to get going and a moment to stop.

Circles

Boxes have corners, and corners catch on things. A circle does not, and the test for two circles is the shortest in the chapter: they overlap when their centres are closer than their two radii added together.

A circle against a rectangle takes one more idea. Find the point of the rectangle nearest to the circle's centre – the centre itself with its x clamped to the rectangle's left and right and its y to its top and bottom – and measure to that:

extends Node

## How far p is from a rectangle: 0 if it is inside.
func to_box(p: Vector2, r: Rect2) -> float:
	var nearest := Vector2(clampf(p.x, r.position.x, r.end.x), clampf(p.y, r.position.y, r.end.y))
	return p.distance_to(nearest)

func _ready() -> void:
	# circle against circle
	var you := Vector2(50, 50)       # radius 10
	var coin := Vector2(62, 59)      # radius 6
	print(you.distance_to(coin), " apart; touching: ", you.distance_to(coin) < 10.0 + 6.0)

	# circle against rectangle
	var table := Rect2(100, 40, 60, 30)
	for p in [Vector2(90, 55), Vector2(130, 55), Vector2(97, 36), Vector2(164, 73)]:
		print(p, " is ", to_box(p, table), " from the table")

Beside the table the answer is the plain sideways gap; inside it is zero; off a corner it is the distance to the corner itself, which is what rounds the corners off. A walker of radius 8 is blocked wherever to_box() is less than 8. Move the coin to (64, 62) and see whether you still reach it.

Build it live

A walker that goes where you click, stops at furniture, slides round it, says when it has arrived and knows when to give up. Six small steps, and at the end you will have written the core of a real game's movement.

In Little Hours

There is not one CharacterBody2D, Area2D or CollisionShape2D in Little Hours. The resident is a plain Node2D, and everything in the second half of this chapter is in its script.

The file is Player.gd, and the same script walks the resident and their partner. What it avoids is decided somewhere else: the room hands it a list of rectangles.

What counts as solid

RoomView.gd draws the furniture, so it is the one that knows where the furniture is. Its solids() turns every piece into a blocking box:

RoomView.gd · lines 714–726

func solids() -> Array[Rect2]:
	var out: Array[Rect2] = []
	for entry in _pieces:
		var obj: Dictionary = entry["obj"]
		if is_flat(str(obj["id"])):
			continue
		var r: Rect2 = entry["rect"]
		var fw: float = r.size.x * FOOT_W_FRAC
		var fh: float = r.size.y * FOOT_H_FRAC
		out.append(Rect2(
			r.position.x + (r.size.x - fw) * 0.5,
			r.end.y - fh,
			fw, fh))

The box is not the whole sprite. It is the middle 82% of its width (FOOT_W_FRAC) and the bottom half of its height (FOOT_H_FRAC): the piece's foot. So you can walk up beside a wardrobe, and stand with your feet behind the top of a sofa where the depth rule from Chapter L draws you behind it, while its base still stops you. Flat things – rugs, mats – are skipped: you walk onto those. The function goes on to add one more rectangle, the water, and its longest comment explains why the small props along the walls are deliberately not solid: one in the wrong place made a neighbour unreachable for a whole release.

Because the boxes are worked out from what is drawn, anything that moves the furniture has to hand out a fresh list. One function in Main.gd does it, for everybody who walks:

Main.gd · lines 1023–1038

## Re-reads the room's blocking boxes. Collision is derived from the *rendered*
## furniture, so anything that moves or re-skins a piece has to call this —
## otherwise the room would block where the art used to be.
func _refresh_solids() -> void:
	var blockers := _room_view.solids()
	# The route must be rebuilt from the same rects, or they will walk around
	# furniture that is no longer there and into furniture that is.
	_rebuild_navmesh()
	_player.solids = blockers
	_partner.solids = blockers
	# The animals get the same rects. They had none, which is why the dog walked
	# through the sofa and the cat crossed the pool.
	_dog.solids = blockers
	_cat.solids = blockers
	if is_instance_valid(_neighbor):
		_neighbor.solids = blockers

Its last comment is a bug report in two sentences. The dog and the cat once had no list, and walked through the sofa. Decorating calls this every time you put a piece down (Decorating), and both animals now use the same test as you do (Animals).

The test and the slide

For collision the resident is "a small disc at their feet", RADIUS := 16.0, and the test is the circle-against-rectangle one, with a helper you have already written:

Player.gd · lines 334–336

## How far `p` is from a box: 0 inside it.
static func _to_box(p: Vector2, r: Rect2) -> float:
	return p.distance_to(Vector2(clampf(p.x, r.position.x, r.end.x), clampf(p.y, r.position.y, r.end.y)))

A point is inside furniture when _to_box(p, r) < RADIUS for any rectangle in solids. Moving is the slide, one axis at a time:

Player.gd · lines 366–379

## Move by `step`, resolving X and Y independently so a diagonal walk into
## furniture keeps whichever component is clear — they slide along the edge
## instead of stopping dead against it.
func _slide(step: Vector2) -> void:
	var p := position
	if step.x != 0.0:
		var nx := _clamped(Vector2(p.x + step.x, p.y))
		if not _in_furniture(nx) and not _bumps(p, nx):
			p.x = nx.x
	if step.y != 0.0:
		var ny := _clamped(Vector2(p.x, p.y + step.y))
		if not _in_furniture(ny) and not _bumps(p, ny):
			p.y = ny.y
	position = p

It is your step 4 with two additions. _clamped() keeps the point inside the room's walkable floor, a Rect2 called bounds. And _bumps() handles the other characters, who are small boxes that change every frame: a step is refused only if it takes you nearer to somebody you are already touching, because, as its comment records, refusing every step near the dog meant that with the dog at your heels you could not move at all.

The speed is not set outright either. Each frame the script works out the velocity it wants – 280 pixels a second towards the next point – and moves the real one towards it with _vel.move_toward(want, rate * delta), quickly when speeding up and more quickly when braking. That gap between wanting and doing is what the comments call weight.

Picking a path

Sliding gets you along a wall. It does not get you round a sofa that is squarely in the way, as the end of the tutorial showed. So the route comes from the one piece of engine machinery the walker does use: a navigation mesh, a map of the walkable floor. _refresh_solids() calls _rebuild_navmesh(), which gives Godot's NavigationServer2D the floor as an outline and each of those same rectangles as a hole in it, and has it work out the walkable area with a 22-pixel margin. When you tap, walk_to() asks that map for a list of points from here to there (NavigationServer2D.map_get_path()), drops the points it can see straight past, and steers at each one in turn. The comment over _rebuild_navmesh() gives the reason for building it from the collision rectangles and nothing else: "what the route avoids and what actually blocks you can never disagree".

The slide is still there underneath, in the code's own words "only a safety net for the last pixel", and so is the give-up timer. Holding a key skips the route altogether: Main reads Input.get_vector() and calls drive(), and the walker goes that way until furniture says no. The whole chain, from a tap to the fridge opening when arrived fires, is in Reaching: tap, walk, act.

That is why the by-hand half of this chapter matters. A real, shipped game moves its characters with a disc, a list of Rect2s, two ifs and move_toward.

Your turn

Six exercises: a tap, a coin, a bug, some bits, the foot of a sofa, and a character to drive.

What to remember

  • CharacterBody2D: set velocity in pixels a second, call move_and_slide() with no arguments in _physics_process. It applies delta itself, and afterwards is_on_floor() tells you where you landed.
  • Area2D notices and does not block: body_entered fires once, on the way in. Bodies and areas both need a CollisionShape2D with a shape.
  • Layer is what an object is; mask is what it looks for. One notices another when its mask names the other's layer.
  • By hand, the world is a list of Rect2s: has_point() for taps, intersects() for boxes, and "nearest point, then distance" for a circle against a box.
  • Test the place you are about to step to, and take the step one axis at a time so a wall refuses only its own half.
  • move_toward arrives without overshooting – for positions, and for velocities when you want weight.
